The moral limits of a market

A price is not
a measure of worth.

Markets help us exchange goods and services. But does the fact that someone is willing to buy—and someone else is willing to sell—make every transaction right? Our answer begins with four boundaries.

01

Your vote

Equal citizenship

A vote is a public trust as well as a personal right. Selling it allows wealth to purchase political power and weakens the equal voice of neighbors who never agreed to the bargain.

02

A child

Personhood before possession

A child is a person to nurture and protect, never property to transfer for profit. Caring for a child creates responsibilities; it does not confer ownership. Adoption provides a family, not a purchase of a human being.

03

A human organ

Bodily integrity

Financial desperation should not become a marketplace for irreversible bodily loss. We support the boundary against organ sales while honoring voluntary donation. U.S. transplant law distinguishes buying an organ from paying permitted donation-related expenses.

04

Your personal freedom

Rights that cannot become property

A person should never become someone else's property—even through an agreement made under financial pressure. We can commit our time and labor without surrendering our personhood or our standing as free and equal people.

The conviction behind this site

Worth is not something
we have to earn.

People are more than what they produce, what someone will pay, or what they can offer us. Their dignity does not rise with their income, fall with their mistakes, or disappear when they are vulnerable.

Freedom matters because people matter. The purpose of a protective boundary should be to preserve human dignity, meaningful choice, and our ability to live together—not to shame people or give government unlimited control.

We want to educate and influence: to examine the beliefs behind our laws and ask when a market serves people—and when it begins to treat people as things.

01
Dignity & the marketplace · 6 minute read

Why do 49 states prohibit buying sex?

The case for limiting a market in sexual access—and why the global picture requires a closer look.

We already accept that some transactions should be off limits. The difficult question is whether buying sexual access belongs among them.

Begin with what the law actually says

Nevada is the only U.S. state with a legal, licensed-brothel exception to the general prohibition on prostitution. The other 49 states prohibit buying sex. Even in Nevada, the exception is limited: state law prohibits purchasing sex outside a licensed house of prostitution, and local restrictions also apply.1 2 That comparison concerns buyers; it does not mean every state treats people selling sex the same way.

The international picture is more varied. UNAIDS reports that 167 countries criminalize some aspect of sex work. That category includes different activities and does not establish that 167 countries—or most countries—specifically criminalize the purchase of sex.3 Some jurisdictions target buyers, others penalize sellers or third parties, and others permit or decriminalize consensual adult transactions.

A legal majority is a starting point for discussion, not proof of moral correctness. Nor should we assume that every legislature adopted its law for the same reason. The argument below is our case for the boundary, rather than a claim about a single historical motive shared by 49 states.

Consent matters. So do the conditions surrounding it.

The strongest argument for allowing the purchase of sex is personal autonomy: adults should generally be free to make choices about their own bodies. That argument deserves a serious answer. Consensual adult sex work and trafficking are not identical, and describing them as interchangeable obscures both agency and coercion.

Our concern is that a transaction can be formally agreed to while taking advantage of another person's severely limited choices. Poverty, dependency, threats, and lack of alternatives can affect bargaining power. These possibilities do not prove that every person selling sex lacks agency. They do require us to ask what a buyer should be permitted to purchase when the consequences fall so intimately on another person.

What does a market teach us to expect?

A market does more than match individual choices; it establishes expectations about what money can obtain. We believe sexual intimacy should remain grounded in freely given, continuing consent, rather than a commercial expectation of access. Payment can never create an entitlement to override a person's boundaries or refusal.

The analogy to votes, children, or organs is about the limits of markets, not a claim that these transactions are legally or morally identical. The common question is whether willingness to pay is enough to justify what is being purchased. Our answer is no.

Why some laws focus on the buyer

Sweden's approach prohibits purchasing sexual services while not criminalizing the person selling them for that act. The Swedish Gender Equality Agency describes its rationale in terms of gender equality, exploitation, and responsibility for demand. It also emphasizes that legislation must be accompanied by social support.4 This is one explicit policy rationale; it should not be presented as the explanation for every country's law.

Our position favors deterring the purchase of sex while treating people in the sex trade with dignity, listening to their experiences, and supporting safety and practical alternatives. A conviction that people are priceless should lead to housing, healthcare, protection from abuse, and opportunities—not contempt.

A protective law must be judged by its effects

The policy debate is real. UNAIDS supports decriminalization and warns that punitive laws can obstruct access to HIV services.5 Supporters of buyer-focused laws must take such concerns seriously, including whether enforcement makes it harder to seek help or report violence. Moral intention alone does not establish that a particular law works.

We believe society should reject a commercial entitlement to sexual access. To honor that principle, policy must protect people in practice, measure outcomes, and respond honestly when enforcement causes harm. The worth of a person cannot depend on agreement with our position.

What should money never entitle one person to expect from another?

02
Liberty & the common good · 6 minute read

Can a law limit a choice and still protect freedom?

Prescription drugs, impaired driving, and organ sales reveal why a free society still needs boundaries.

America should give people wide room to direct their own lives. But maximizing freedom is not the same as permitting every individual action.

We share roads, families, healthcare systems, and institutions. Some choices impose serious risks on people who never consented to them. Others can permanently damage the very capacity to make meaningful choices in the future. A society committed to liberty must consider both.

A personal decision can impose a public danger

Driving while intoxicated makes the issue clear. Choosing to drink does not entitle someone to expose pedestrians, passengers, or other drivers to an unreasonable risk. NHTSA explains that alcohol impairs the abilities needed for safe driving and that every state prohibits driving at or above a specified blood-alcohol threshold.1

We need not wait for a collision before setting a boundary. A narrowly drawn prohibition can deter dangerous conduct while preserving ordinary travel. The principle is not that government owns the driver; it is that other people have rights too.

Medical safeguards can protect the person choosing

Taking prescription medication without an appropriate prescription can bypass safeguards around diagnosis, dose, interactions, and supervision. FDA guidance says not to use another person's prescription medicine.2 In our view, requiring appropriate authorization for prescription medicines is a reasonable way to protect people from risks they may be poorly equipped to evaluate alone.

Legal precision matters here. Federal law generally prohibits unauthorized possession of controlled substances, with exceptions including valid prescriptions and other lawful authorization.3 That does not mean every misuse of every prescription medicine is the same federal crime. Safety regulation, medical treatment, and criminal punishment are different tools; the appropriate response depends on the conduct and the law.

Some bargains can undermine future freedom

Selling a transplant organ raises a different concern: an irreversible bodily loss in return for money. Federal law prohibits transferring covered human organs for valuable consideration for transplantation when the transfer affects interstate commerce. It permits specified donation-related expense payments and provides an exception for qualifying paired donation.4 This is not a blanket prohibition on compensation for every substance or material from the human body.

The moral case for this restriction is that economic need should not make bodily integrity a commodity available to the highest bidder. The strongest objection is that voluntary compensation might expand supply and respect a donor's choice. That tradeoff deserves scrutiny. Our position is that supporting donation and meeting financial need are better goals than making organ sales an ordinary market.

Freedom should not include becoming property

The distinction is sharpest when someone proposes selling away their own freedom. We believe a society should refuse to enforce an agreement that makes a person another person's property. Protecting the ability to choose tomorrow can justify refusing a transaction today. America's Thirteenth Amendment prohibits slavery and involuntary servitude, subject to its stated exception for punishment following criminal conviction.5

Selling a vote illustrates another shared interest. Federal law prohibits paying or accepting payment for voting in elections covered by the statute.6 Our reason for defending that boundary is equal citizenship: a private bargain can corrupt an institution that belongs to everyone.

The burden of justification stays with the law

These examples support protective laws, not unlimited control. Something being unwise, offensive, or unpopular does not automatically justify prohibition. Before limiting a choice, lawmakers should identify a serious harm, show why the measure is likely to reduce it, consider less restrictive alternatives, and weigh the harms of enforcement.

Deterrence is a purpose, not a guaranteed result. Effective policy may combine clear rules with education, treatment, support, and proportionate penalties. It should remain open to revision when evidence shows that another approach better protects people.

Our standard is freedom with human dignity: enough restraint to protect people from exploitation and serious harm, and enough humility to keep protective power from becoming oppressive. Because people are priceless, both the conduct we regulate and the way we enforce the law deserve careful judgment.

Does this boundary preserve people's ability to live freely—or take more freedom than it protects?
